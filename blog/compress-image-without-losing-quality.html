How to Compress an Image Without Losing Quality

Smaller files that still look sharp are possible. Here is what is really happening and the settings to use.

By the Image Compressor Now teamPublished 2026-11-12Updated 2026-11-137 min read
Short answer

Perfectly identical copies are only possible with lossless formats like PNG, but you can usually shrink a photo a lot with no visible change. Use WebP or JPEG at about 75 to 85 percent quality, limit the width to the size you actually display, and compare the result with the original at 100 percent zoom. Always keep the original file.

Everyone wants a smaller file that looks exactly the same. The honest answer has two parts: a mathematically identical copy is only possible in a few cases, but a copy that looks identical is easy to get. This guide explains the difference and gives you the settings that work for most photos and graphics.

What "without losing quality" really means

There are two families of compression. Lossless compression shrinks a file in a way that can be reversed exactly, so every pixel comes back unchanged. PNG works like this. Lossy compression, used by JPEG, WebP and AVIF, throws away detail the eye is unlikely to notice, which makes much smaller files but cannot be undone.

So when people say "no quality loss", they usually mean no visible loss. That is a realistic goal, and it is where the big savings are.

The sweet spot is 75 to 85 percent quality

Quality is not a straight line. Going from 100% to 85% often cuts a photo's size dramatically while the picture looks the same to nearly everyone. Going from 85% to 40% saves less and starts to show blocky patches. For photos, 75 to 85% in JPEG or WebP is the best starting range. Quality 100% is still lossy, only very cautious, and it can produce a file several times larger than 85% for no visible gain.

The biggest win is usually dimensions

A modern phone photo can be 4000 pixels wide or more. If it appears 1200 pixels wide on a web page, the extra pixels are wasted bytes. Setting a maximum width that matches where the image is shown removes that waste without touching the look of the picture at its real display size. Never enlarge a small image, because that adds bytes and no detail.

Step by step: shrink an image and keep it looking the same

  1. Add your image to the image compressor. Use the original file, not one that was already compressed.
  2. Pick WebP or JPEG. WebP is usually smaller. Google's study measured lossy WebP at 25 to 34 percent smaller than comparable JPEGs. Choose JPEG if the image must open in every program.
  3. Set quality to about 80%.
  4. Set a maximum width equal to the largest size the image is displayed, for example 1600 px for a full-width banner or 800 px for a picture inside an article.
  5. Compare at 100% zoom. Look at smooth gradients such as skies, at skin and at the edges of text.
  6. Lower quality in steps of 5. Stop one step before you can see a difference, and keep that setting for similar images.

What quality loss actually looks like

Knowing the signs helps you stop at the right setting. Watch for:

  • Blocky patches in flat areas, a sign that quality is too low.
  • Banding in skies and gradients, where smooth colour turns into visible steps.
  • Halos or fuzz around text and sharp edges, common when graphics are saved as JPEG.
  • Mushy skin or fur where fine texture has been smoothed away.

Judge at the size people will really see. A flaw that only appears when you zoom to 400 percent rarely matters on a web page.

Mistakes that cost quality for nothing

  • Compressing a compressed copy. Each lossy pass discards more detail. Always start again from the original.
  • Enlarging before compressing. Upscaling cannot create detail and makes the file bigger.
  • Saving text graphics as JPEG. Use PNG or WebP instead.
  • Chasing a target size with quality alone. Reduce dimensions first, as explained in our guide to compressing an image to 100 KB or 200 KB.

Does removing metadata hurt quality?

No. Exif metadata such as camera model and GPS location sits beside the pixels, not in them. Our tool drops it during re-encoding, which saves a few bytes and removes location data, and it applies the picture's rotation first so the image still faces the right way.

Keep your originals

Lossy compression cannot be reversed. Save compressed copies under a new name and keep the original, so you can always redo the job with different settings. If a result ever comes out larger, see why a compressed image can be bigger than the original.

Quick answers

Can I compress an image with no quality loss at all?

Only with a lossless format such as PNG, and the savings are usually modest. For photos, lossy compression at 75 to 85 percent gives much smaller files that look the same at normal viewing size.

What quality setting looks the same as the original?

For JPEG and WebP photos, roughly 75 to 85 percent. Test your own image, because detailed or noisy pictures need a slightly higher value than simple ones.

Is WebP better than JPEG for keeping quality?

At the same visual quality WebP files are typically smaller. Google's study measured 25 to 34 percent smaller than JPEG. Use JPEG when you need universal compatibility.

Does resizing reduce quality?

Shrinking an image to the size it is displayed does not reduce the quality people see, and it is the most effective way to cut file size. Enlarging it does reduce sharpness.

Why does my screenshot look blurry after compression?

It was probably saved as JPEG. Sharp text and lines suit PNG or high-quality WebP better.
